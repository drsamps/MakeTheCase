## Case Synopsis

Set in the 1970s, this case describes the development of Benihana of Tokyo, a limited-menu restaurant chain created by young Japanese entrepreneur Rocky Aoki. The firm has grown to fifteen restaurants, five of which are franchised, and faces several alternatives for future growth including branching into new product concepts and geographic areas.

---

## Learning Objectives

Students will:
- Understand how a well-designed operating system fits into competitive strategy and delivers superior financial performance
- Learn the origins of the limited-menu restaurant concept and its strategic benefits
- Gain insight into Benihana's cost advantages through numerical analysis
- Master process analysis concepts including process flow diagrams, batching, inventory, cycle time, and throughput time
- Recognize how separate elements of an operating system integrate to increase profitability
- Reflect on how to leverage operational strengths for business expansion

---

## Assignment Questions

1. What are the differences between the Benihana production process and that of a typical restaurant?
2. Examine the production system in detail. What are the major design choices which generate operating efficiencies?

## Key Teaching Points

### I. The Benihana Concept

**Food Quality and Presentation**
- 46.7% of customers cite good food as primary reason for visiting
- 77.9% of customers said food was excellent
- Limited menu selections: only 4 meat choices (steak, filet mignon, chicken, shrimp)
- Side dishes cannot be changed (bean sprouts, zucchini, mushrooms, onions, rice)
- Familiar food in exotic surroundings
- Small portions (only 45.4% rated portions as excellent)

**Entertainment Value**
- Emphasis on showmanship in table-side cooking
- Opportunity to see food cooked by highly trained native Japanese chef
- Chef serves customer instead of wait person, delivering faster service
- 63.2% of customers rated atmosphere as excellent
- Fun place to eat with authentic, informal environment

**Pricing and Efficiency**
- Moderate prices: approximately $6 at lunch, $10 at dinner
- Quick meal: average turnover time 1 hour for dinner
- Good space utilization: only 22% of floor space devoted to preparation/storage (vs. 30% for typical restaurants)

### II. Cost Structure Comparison with Typical Sit-Down Restaurants

**Food Costs**
- Benihana: 30%-35% of food sales
- Typical restaurant: 38%-48% of food sales
- Limited menu leads to high product standardization and less demand variability
- Easier to predict aggregate demand
- Less inventory, less spoilage, lower storage costs
- Ability to purchase large quantities creates purchasing economies
- Minimal advance food preparation reduces labor requirements
- Small portions with high vegetable proportion (cheaper than meat) create illusion of more food
- High restaurant revenues per dollar of food cost generates high economic efficiency

**Beverage Costs**
- Benihana: 20% of beverage sales
- Typical restaurant: 25%-30% of beverage sales
- Higher priced exotic drinks made from rum, gin, vodka (rather than costlier bourbon/scotch)
- Percentage of alcohol in exotic drinks can be lowered without affecting taste (weaker drinks)

**Labor Costs**
- Benihana: 10%-12% of gross sales
- Typical restaurant: 30%-35% of gross sales
- Number of trained chefs similar to typical restaurant (6-8 chefs out of 30 total employees = 20%-25%)
- Benihana chefs are more productive:
  - Perform multiple functions: bring food to table, cut food for customers, cook, clean up
  - Cook quickly (part of entertainment, rapid precision cutting)
  - Reduced meal time allows more tables served per day/evening
  - Higher customer turnover generates higher restaurant revenues per hour
  - Little advance food preparation allows more time servicing customers
  - All chefs have customer contact, directly contributing to food sales
  - Chefs receive tips, allowing lower salaries
  - High labor productivity: 1 chef and 1 wait person serve 2 tables of 8 people each
  - High restaurant revenues per dollar of labor cost generates high economic efficiency
  - Fewer wait people, bus people, dishwashers needed (no pots and pans to clean)

**Advertising Costs**
- Benihana: 8%-10% of gross sales
- Typical restaurant: 0.75%-2.0% of gross sales
- High for restaurant industry, but not for entertainment industry
- Selling more than food; advertisements don't use word "restaurant"
- Highlighting entertainment aspects and fun atmosphere
- Promoting unfamiliar Japanese food to uninformed consumers
- Aimed at tourist business

**Rent Costs**
- Benihana: 5%-7% of gross sales
- Typical restaurant: 4.5%-9% of gross sales
- Benihana on lower end of typical range
- Benihana devotes 22% of floor space to preparation/storage (vs. 30% typical)
- More space devoted to direct customer service
- More space available for generating sales
- Communal dining at hibachi table always fills to capacity of 8
- Higher seat utilization generates higher restaurant revenues per square foot
- Sited in high-traffic locations: business districts for lunch (30%-40% of sales), tourist areas, impulse diners

### III. Process Flow on a Busy Night

**Customer Arrival and Seating**
- Customers funneled into bar unless in party of 8 with available table
- Different size groups arrive at different times; bar acts as storage until batch of 8 can be seated
- Customers have drinks before dinner, increasing beverage sales and restaurant revenues
- Limited menu helps unfamiliar customers order quickly
- Wait person doesn't need to travel to kitchen; hibachi table is the kitchen

**Food Preparation**
- Minimal food preparation done in advance of customer arrival
- Customer orders processed and assembled simultaneously by placing raw food on tray and transporting to hibachi table
- Limited menu reduces variety of preparatory work required

**Cooking and Eating**
- Chef brings raw food on tray
- Chef works quickly to impress diners with cutting speed and skill (increases tips)
- Chef cuts food into bite-size pieces so customers can eat more quickly
- Smaller meat pieces cook faster
- Chef paces meal and show:
  - Signals meal/show beginning by starting to cook
  - Signals meal/show ending by cleaning table and shutting off overhead fan/light
- On busy nights, chef paces meal/show more quickly to maximize customers served and tip total
- On slow nights, chef paces meal/show more slowly to provide more "service," allow more drink orders from bar, and maximize tip per table
- Watching meal cook creates anticipation, sharpens appetite, encourages quick eating
- All customers watching chef and show discourages conversation during meal

**Dessert Service**
- Ice cream (fried ginger or green tea ice cream)
- Easy to prepare ahead; doesn't spoil easily; cheaper than pastries
- Ginger and green tea exotic but safe and familiar to Americans
- Melts quickly so customers must eat quickly
- Communal seating discourages lingering after meal
- No incentive to talk with strangers at table
- Seats not well-padded; uncomfortable after long periods
- Dessert arrival signals show is over
- Chef says good-byes
- Overhead fan/light turned off
- Table is cleaned
- Check brought to table
- Only need one person to leave to signal others it's time to depart

**Customer Exit**
- Customer exit path through cocktail lounge/bar
- Potential for after-dinner beverage sales

## Suggested Answers

### Question 1: Differences Between Benihana and Typical Restaurant Production Processes

**Key Differences:**

1. **Limited vs. Extensive Menu**
   - Benihana: 4 meat choices with fixed side dishes
   - Typical restaurant: extensive menu with numerous variations and preparations

2. **Preparation Timing**
   - Benihana: minimal advance food preparation
   - Typical restaurant: extensive advance preparation (sauces, cuts, stocks, etc.)

3. **Cooking Location**
   - Benihana: hibachi table is cooking location (table-side, visible to customers)
   - Typical restaurant: kitchen (hidden from customers)

4. **Chef Roles**
   - Benihana: chefs perform multiple functions (cooking, serving, cutting, cleaning)
   - Typical restaurant: clear separation of roles (chef, server, busser)

5. **Customer Seating**
   - Benihana: communal seating (8 people per table, strangers together)
   - Typical restaurant: individual party seating

6. **Pacing Control**
   - Benihana: chef controls pacing and meal timing
   - Typical restaurant: server controls pacing

7. **Entertainment Component**
   - Benihana: high emphasis on showmanship as integral part of product
   - Typical restaurant: minimal entertainment value

### Question 2: Major Design Choices Generating Operating Efficiencies

**Food Cost Efficiencies:**
- Limited menu design enables:
  - High product standardization
  - Predictable demand
  - Large-quantity purchasing (economies of scale)
  - Minimal advance preparation (reduced labor)
  - Small portions with vegetable filler (lower ingredient costs)

**Labor Cost Efficiencies:**
- Multi-functional chef design enables:
  - Fewer specialized staff required
  - Higher productivity per chef
  - Tips compensating for lower salaries
  - Direct customer contact generates higher sales contribution
  - Reduced cooking time increases table turnover
  - Fewer support staff (no dishwashers for cooking equipment)

**Revenue Generation Efficiencies:**
- Communal seating design ensures:
  - 100% table capacity utilization (always 8 people)
  - Higher seat utilization rate
  - More tables turned per day/evening
  - Higher total restaurant revenues

**Space Utilization Efficiencies:**
- Hibachi cooking method enables:
  - Only 22% floor space for preparation/storage (vs. 30% typical)
  - More dining space
  - Higher revenues per square foot

**Beverage Revenue Efficiencies:**
- Bar batching system enables:
  - Customers waiting in bar consume drinks
  - Increased beverage sales
  - Higher total restaurant revenues
  - Higher profit margins on beverages (20% food cost vs. typical 25%-30%)

**Strategic Integration:**
- All design choices support core concept: entertainment and experience over traditional food service
- Operational efficiencies directly enable:
  - Moderate pricing strategy
  - Superior profitability
  - Competitive advantage

## Numerical Analysis Examples

### IV. Calculation of Chef's Annual Wages in Chicago Unit

**Given Data:**
- Gross sales: $1,300,000/year
- Labor cost: 10% of gross sales = $130,000/year
- Restaurant requires 30 people:
  - 1 manager: $15,000/year
  - 1 assistant manager: $12,000/year
  - 2 front men: $9,000/year each = $18,000/year
  - 20 workers and 6 chefs share remaining labor budget

**Calculations:**

Managerial wages: $15,000 + $12,000 + $18,000 = $45,000/year

Remaining for 26 nonmanagerial workers: $130,000 - $45,000 = $85,000/year

Average nonmanagerial wage: $85,000 ÷ 26 = $3,269/year

Assuming nonchef workers earn $4,000/year:
- 20 workers × $4,000 = $80,000
- 6 chefs share remaining $5,000 = $833/year base salary per chef

Tips distributed: 20% of gross sales = $260,000/year
- Assume 15 nonmanagerial employees (including chefs) share tips equally
- Tips per employee: $260,000 ÷ 15 = $17,333/year

**Chef's Total Annual Compensation:**
- Base salary: $833
- Tips (estimated): $17,333
- **Total: approximately $18,166**

**Key Insight:**
- Tips approximately double chef's base salary
- Salary structure: company pays 1/3 of chef's annual wages, customers pay 2/3 (via tips)
- Chef take-home highly dependent on tips—strong incentive to service as many tables as possible
- Despite tips, chef compensation remains below managerial workers

**Note:** These figures are from early 1970s. Multiply by 3 to estimate today's equivalent values.

### V. Calculation of Profit After Tax for Chicago Unit

**Revenue Breakdown:**
- Gross sales: $1,300,000/year
- Food sales (70%): $910,000/year
- Beverage sales (30%): $390,000/year

**Cost Structure:**
- Food costs (30% of food sales): $273,000
- Beverage costs (20% of beverage sales): $78,000
- Labor (nonmanagerial, 10% of gross): $130,000
- Managerial wages: $45,000
- Advertising (10% of gross): $130,000
- Rent (5% of gross): $65,000

**Profit Calculation:**
- Pre-tax profit: $1,300,000 - $273,000 - $78,000 - $130,000 - $45,000 - $130,000 - $65,000 = **$579,000**
- Assuming 50% tax rate
- **Profit after tax: $289,500/year**

**Return on Investment:**
- Initial investment per unit: $300,000
- Annual PAT: $289,500
- Return on investment (first year): 96.5%
- Payback period: ~1.25 years

**Key Insight:**
Benihana's operating system design translates to superior profitability despite higher advertising costs.

### VI. Calculation of Bar Wait Time (Maximum Throughput Time) on Busy Night

**Benihana West:**
- Bar seating: 8 seats
- Dining seating: 40 seats
- Bar is 20% of total seating

Calculation:
- Dining tables: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 tables
- Individual table cycle time: 60 minutes
- Dining area cycle time per table: 60 ÷ 5 = 12 minutes/table
- Bar wait time: 1 table × 12 minutes = **12 minutes**

**Benihana East:**
- Bar seating: 16 seats
- Dining seating: 80 seats
- Bar is 20% of total seating

Calculation:
- Dining tables: 80 ÷ 8 = 10 tables
- Dining area cycle time per table: 60 ÷ 10 = 6 minutes/table
- Bar wait time: 2 tables × 6 minutes = **12 minutes**

**Benihana Palace (Standardized Restaurant):**
- Bar seating: 56 seats
- Dining seating: 120 seats
- Bar is 47% of total seating

Calculation:
- Dining tables: 120 ÷ 8 = 15 tables
- Dining area cycle time per table: 60 ÷ 15 = 4 minutes/table
- Bar wait time: 7 tables × 4 minutes = **28 minutes**

**Key Insights:**
- 12 minutes in bar = time for one to two drinks
- 28 minutes in bar = time for two to three drinks (potentially four)
- Larger restaurants with more dining tables have shorter individual cycle times but longer total bar wait times
- Bar wait time directly increases beverage revenue
- Process design manages inventory (customers waiting in bar) to maximize throughput and revenue

### Instructional Approach

**Opening Activity (5 minutes):**
- Poll class: Who has eaten at Benihana?
- **Show videotape of four 1970s Benihana commercials (4 minutes, 26 seconds)**
  - Introduces chefs, customers, Rocky, restaurant operations, marketing concept
  - Context note: Remind students these are from 1970s; American public much more sophisticated today about Japanese food and culture
  - Alternative if videotape unavailable: Have students examine Exhibits 5-9 in case

**First Discussion Block: The Benihana Concept (15-20 minutes)**
- Launch with: "What is the Benihana concept?"
- Listen for student responses matching Analysis Section I
- Transition: "Now that we understand what Benihana is selling, how does their cost structure contribute to success?"

**Second Discussion Block: Cost Structure (20-25 minutes)**
- Review major points from first block
- Ask: "How does Benihana's cost structure contribute to its financial success?"
- Highlight food, beverage, labor, advertising, and rent cost advantages
- **Perform calculations:**
  - Chef's annual wages (Analysis Section IV)
  - Profit after tax for Chicago unit (Analysis Section V)
- Discuss how cost structure enables financial performance

**Third Discussion Block: Operations and Process Flow (25-30 minutes)**
- Transition: "At this point, you've probably noticed Benihana's cost structure is intimately linked to its process flow design."
- Ask: "What is the process flow on a typical busy night?"
- **Construct detailed process flow diagram on board (Exhibit TN-2)** as discussion progresses
- Define operations terms as they surface:
  - Input/output
  - Inventory or storage
  - Capacity
  - Throughput time
  - Batching
  - Batch or lot size
  - Cycle time
  - Bottleneck
  - Setup time
  - Run time
- Perform bar cycle time calculation (Analysis Section VI)
- Focus discussion on meal pacing:
  - Ask: "Who paces the meal?"
  - Ask: "What is the throughput time for a batch of customers and how does Benihana achieve these times?"
  - Ask: "How does Benihana get customers to eat quickly without making them feel rushed?"
  - Ask: "How is the dining experience paced at Benihana?"

**Closure and Update (5-10 minutes)**
- Summarize key insights
- **Update: Rocky's subsequent venture - Big Splash (1985)**
  - Seafood restaurant in Miami
  - Completely different operating system
  - Wide selection of seafood (20 different preparation styles)
  - Result: Long waits, customer complaints, operational problems
  - Key lesson: When Rocky moved away from limited-menu concept, operational problems emerged
- Final synthesis:
  - Benihana examined through three lenses: marketing concept, cost structure, operations
  - Well-designed operating system supports business strategy
  - Integration with technology choice (hibachi cooking) and product (good food/show/exotic setting)
  - Operations deliver superior financial performance
  - To understand financial performance, understand the operating system

### Key Teaching Notes

**Pedagogical Insights:**
- Process flow diagram is not just an analysis tool but an excellent communication tool
- Use videotape if available to help students who haven't experienced Benihana
- Calculations (chef wages, PAT, bar cycle time) reinforce how operations translate to financial performance
- The integration of all design elements (menu, cooking method, seating, staffing) is critical insight
- Students should recognize that every operational choice supports the central concept and financial model

**Common Student Misconceptions to Address:**
- Benihana is primarily a restaurant (vs. entertainment experience with food)
- Menu limitation is a constraint (vs. strategic enabler)
- Tips are just customer generosity (vs. intentional incentive system)
- Process flow analysis is irrelevant to profitability (vs. fundamental driver)

**Discussion Management:**
- Ensure students recognize links between:
  - Limited menu → cost savings AND operational efficiency
  - Chef compensation structure → incentive for high productivity
  - Communal seating → capacity utilization and revenue generation
  - Bar design → beverage revenue optimization
  - Pace control → throughput maximization

**Real-World Connection:**
- Big Splash example demonstrates that operational systems cannot be transplanted without fundamental business concept
- Different business models require fundamentally different operational designs
- Operating system must be aligned with strategy and technology

"Now let's understand exactly what Benihana is selling and why customers choose to eat there."
