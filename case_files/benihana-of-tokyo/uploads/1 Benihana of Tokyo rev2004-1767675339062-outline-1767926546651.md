## Executive Overview

### Key Quote
> "Some restaurateurs like myself have more fun than others," says Hiroaki (Rocky) Aoki, youthful president of Benihana of Tokyo.

### Company Performance (1964-1972)
- Started with deficit net worth in 1964
- By 1972: President of 15-restaurant chain
- Gross annual revenue: Over $12 million
- Growth period: 8 years from single 40-seat unit to established chain

## Background and History

### Company Founding
- **Original Founder:** Yunosuke Aoki (Rocky's father, "Papasan")
- **Original Opening:** 1935 in Japan
- **Original Restaurant Name:** Benihana
  - Named after small red flower growing wild near the restaurant's front door
- **Hibachi Concept Introduction:** 1958
  - Implemented by Papasan due to rising costs and increased competition in Japan

### Rocky Aoki's Journey to America
- **Year Arrived:** 1959
- **Original Context:** Tour with university wrestling team
- **Age:** 20 years old
- **Initial Reaction:** "When I reached New York, it was love at first sight!"
- **Education:** Enrolled in School of Restaurant Management at City College
- **Early Employment:** Washing dishes, driving ice cream truck, acting as tour guide

### Rocky's Market Analysis (3-year systematic study)
- Americans enjoy eating in exotic surroundings but are mistrustful of exotic foods
- People enjoy watching their food being prepared
- Number one problem in U.S. restaurant industry: availability and cost of labor

### First Unit Opening
- **Location:** West Side of Manhattan
- **Year:** 1964 (not 1965)
- **Initial Capital:** 
  - $10,000 saved
  - $20,000 borrowed
  - Total: $30,000
- **Seating Capacity:** 40 seats
- **Success Metric:** Paid for itself in six months

## Operational Model and Strategic Advantages

### Core Concept
- Steakhouse with unique differentiation
- Food cooked in front of customer by Japanese chefs
- Decor: Authentically detailed Japanese country inn

### Labor Cost Advantages
- **Target Labor Cost:** 10%-12% of gross sales (food and beverage)
- **Key Advantage:** Elimination of conventional kitchen with hibachi table arrangement
- **Service Delivery:** Unusual amount of attentive service maintained despite low labor costs
- **Space Efficiency:** Only 22% of total space devoted to back-of-house operations
  - Preparation areas
  - Dry and refrigerated storage
  - Employee dressing rooms
  - Office space
  - **Industry Standard:** 30% back-of-house space

### Food Cost Control
- **Menu Strategy:** Limited to three simple Middle American entrees
  - Steak
  - Chicken
  - Shrimp
- **Target Food Cost:** 30%-35% of food sales
- **Waste Reduction:** Virtually no waste achieved through limited menu
- **Key Advantage:** Reduced food storage and wastage overhead

### Authenticity Commitment
- All walls, ceilings, beams, artifacts, and decorative lights sourced from Japan
- Building materials: Gathered from old Japanese houses
- Process: Carefully disassembled and shipped in pieces to United States
- Assembly: Reassembled by Japanese carpenter crews
- **Cost Implication:** Significant expense requiring continuous evaluation

## Restaurant Growth and Expansion Timeline

### Unit Development (1964-1972)

#### New York Locations
1. **Benihana West (West Side)**
   - Opened: 1964
   - Seating: 40 original
   - Bar capacity: ~8 seats
   - Lounge area: None
   - Beverage sales: ~18% of total sales

2. **Benihana East (East Side)**
   - Opened: 1966
   - Location: Three blocks from West location
   - Original purpose: Cater to West overflow
   - Outcome: Developed separate clientele, prospered independently
   - Bar capacity: Doubled from West
   - Beverage sales: 20%-22% of total sales

3. **Benihana Palace**
   - Opened: 1970
   - Bar/lounge area: Enormous (larger ratio than previous units)
   - Beverage sales: 30%-33% of total sales
   - Beverage cost: Average 20% of beverage sales

  - Circumstance: Barron Hilton approached Rocky; deal made opportunistically
  - Largest money-maker for company
  - Annual gross: ~$1.3 million

  - Expense percentages: Food (30%), Labor (10%), Advertising (10%), Management (4%), Rent (5%)

- **San Francisco:** Fourth unit
- **Encino, California**

### Franchise Strategy and Reversal

#### Franchised Units (1969-1970)
- **Total Franchises Granted:** 6
- **Locations:**
  - Boston
  - Fort Lauderdale
  - Beverly Hills
  - Seattle
  - Harrisburg, Pennsylvania
  - Puerto Rico (unsuccessful due to economic downturn)
  - Portland (later purchased back by company)

#### Franchising Decision Reversal (1970)
- **Decision:** Ceased granting new franchises
- **Rationale - Problem 1:** Franchisees were investors with no restaurant experience
- **Rationale - Problem 2:** Difficulty for American investors to relate to predominantly native Japanese staff
- **Rationale - Problem 3:** Control considerably more difficult with franchisees than company employee managers
- **Strategic Benefit:** Competition not easily replicable
  - Imitators (including one with intimate Benihana knowledge) attempted to compete nearby
  - Competitors folded within the year
  - Confidence established that rapid franchising to preempt competitors was unnecessary

## Restaurant Operations

### Menu and Pricing

#### Menu Items
- Four food items available:
  - Steak
  - Filet mignon
  - Chicken
  - Shrimp
- **Presentation Options:** Single entree items or combinations
- **Full Dinner Composition:** Three items with shrimp as appetizer
- **Standard Accompaniments:** Bean sprouts, zucchini, fresh mushrooms, onions, and rice

#### Meat Specifications
- Grade: U.S.D.A. Prime Grade only
- Cuts: Tightly specified tenderloin and boneless strip loins
- In-house Processing: Further trimmed; only fat at tail left
- Presentation: Chef dramatically trims tail and pushes aside before cubing meat

#### Pricing
- **Lunch Average Check:** ~$6 (including food and beverage)
  - Includes one drink (average $1.50)
  - Lower portions and fewer combinations
- **Dinner Average Check:** ~$10 (including food and beverage)
  - Includes average of one-plus drink

#### Revenue Mix
- Lunch business: 30%-40% of total dollar volume despite lower check average
- Same menu items served at lunch and dinner (different portion sizes)

### Service Model

#### Teppanyaki Table
- **Table Construction:** Gas-fired steel griddle plate with 9½" wooden ledge bordering
  - Ledge holds customers' plates and silverware
- **Exhaust System:** Hood above every table to remove cooking steam, odors, and heat
- **Capacity:** Eight diners per table
- **Staffing:** Chef and waitress per table; each team handles two regular tables

#### Dining Experience Duration
- **Standard Meal Time:** 45 minutes (excluding bar time) if needed
- **Average Table Turnover:** One hour
- **Slow Period Turnover:** Up to one hour and a half
- **Service Flow:**
  1. Customer seated at table of eight
  2. Waitress takes order and brings soup, salad, beverages
  3. Chef appears with food cart
  4. Show begins

### Hours of Operation
- All units open for lunch and dinner
- Specific hours vary by local requirements
- Not necessarily open for every meal every day

## Staff and Training

### Employee Composition (Per Unit)
- **Total per unit:** Approximately 30 people (all Oriental)
- **Highly trained chefs:** 6-8
- **Waitresses:** Similar number to chefs
- **Managers and front men:** 4-5
- **Bar staff:** 2-3 people
- **Support staff:** Bus staff and dishwashers (remainder)

### Chef Training Program

#### Selection Criteria
- **Age:** Young
- **Family Status:** Single
- **Nationality:** Native Japanese
- **Certification:** Completed three-year formal apprenticeship

#### U.S. Training Process
- **Duration:** 3-6 months in Japan
- **Components:**
  - English language instruction
  - American manners training
  - Benihana cooking form (mostly showmanship)
- **Entry Method:** Brought to United States under trade treaty agreement

#### Continuous Training in U.S.
- **Internal Competition:** Chefs compete to perfect art in hopes of becoming chief chef
- **Quality Control:** Traveling chef inspects each unit periodically
- **Support:** Traveling chef involved in grand openings of new units

### Chef Retention Factors

#### Japanese Recruitment Challenges
- General level of prosperity in Japan
- Competition from other restaurants bidding for talents
- Difficulty attracting employees from Japan

#### U.S. Retention Advantages
- **Career Advancement:** Rapidity of advancement versus rigid Japanese hierarchy (based on class, age, education)
- **Compensation Structure (Tangible):** Well-paid in direct financial terms
- **Compensation Structure (Intangible):** Large portion based on:
  - Job security
  - Total commitment of Benihana to employee well-being
  - Paternalistic approach

#### Turnover and Return
- Very low U.S. turnover
- Most eventually return to Japan
- **Strategic Context:** Unique combination of Japanese paternalism in American setting crucial to success

### Organizational Hierarchy

#### Unit Level
- **Manager:** $15,000/year

- **Vice President, Operations and Business Development:** Bill Susha (Saito reports to him)
- **Corporate Staff:**
  - Controller
  - Advertising/Public Relations Director (Glen Simoes)

#### Leadership Context
- **Key Personnel:** Company essentially run by three people—Rocky, Bill Susha, and Allen Saito
- **Note:** Careful balance between control system needs and overhead sustainability

### Control Systems

#### System Implementation
- **Introduced by:** Bill Susha (joined 1971)
- **Susha's Background:** Food and beverage experience with Hilton, Loew's, and Flagship Hotel Division of American Airlines
- **Susha's Priority:** Management growth as objective

#### Specific Control Mechanisms
- **Sales Goals and Budgets:** Established structured goal-setting
- **Projection Process:** Managers project annual sales goals, broken into:
  - Monthly
  - Weekly
  - Daily
  - Individual quota figures negotiated with V.P.

#### Bonus Plan Structure
- **Trigger:** Any unit exceeding quota on any basis (daily, weekly, monthly, yearly)
- **Allocation:** Proportionate bonus prorated across entire unit staff
- **Objective:** Incentivize unit performance

#### Accounting Infrastructure
- **Built-up:** Accounting staff and controller position created
- **Function:** Monitor costs
- **Approach:** Slow but steady process
- **Constraint:** Balance control needs with sustainable overhead

### Management Philosophy

#### Susha's Perspective
> "I see management growth as a priority objective... It's been a slow but steady process. We have to be very careful to balance our need for control with the amount of overhead we can stand."

> "We may be able to justify extra front men standing around in the units if they improve service and bring in more business. At the corporate level, however, we have to be very careful."

## Future Growth Strategy and Challenges

### Expansion Constraints

#### Production Capacity Limitation
- **Current Capacity:** Can open only 5 units per year
- **Constraint:** Limited by two crews of Japanese carpenters available
- **Implication:** Japanese carpenters unable to work faster

#### Capital Requirements
- **Cost per Unit:** Minimum of $300,000
- **Primary Cost Driver:** Authentic Japanese construction materials and carpentry

#### Staffing Challenges
- **Key Constraint:** Need for approximately 30 Oriental employees per unit
- **Difficulty:** Sourcing qualified staff in American market
- **Competition:** International demand for trained Japanese hospitality staff

### Geographic Expansion Strategy

#### Bill Susha's Expansion Vision
- **Immediate Focus:** Major cities only
  - Atlanta
  - Dallas
  - St. Louis

- **Secondary Phase:** Suburban expansion from major market bases

### Failed Expansion Attempts

#### Franchise Program
- **Decision:** Discontinued program
- **Reasons:** Franchisees were businessmen seeking investment opportunities without restaurant experience; Japanese staff obligations unmet by franchisees; control difficult with franchisees; greater profitability found in company operations

#### Outside Investment
- **Opportunity:** Large international banking organization offered major investment
- **Outcome:** Rejected
- **Rationale:** Unacceptable amount of control and autonomy required to be surrendered
- **Context:** "We've been highly tempted to try to grow too fast without really considering the full implications of the move."

### Operational Model Reconsideration

#### Authenticity vs. Speed/Cost Trade-off
> "Another thing I'm considering is whether it's worth it to import from Japan every item used on construction to make a Benihana 100% authentic. Does an American really appreciate it and is it worth the cost?"

**Alternative Consideration:**
- Use locally available materials to achieve substantially same effect
- Reduce costs significantly
- Allow faster expansion

#### Labor Cost Reconsideration
- Question: Is it worth using Japanese carpenters and paying union carpenters to sit and watch?
- Potential impact: Could reduce costs tremendously and allow faster expansion

### Going Public Consideration

### Strategic Risks and Considerations

#### Replication Risk
- Attempted imitations failed (folded within year)
- Creates confidence in competitive advantage
- But: Does not eliminate all expansion-related risks

#### Control vs. Growth Trade-off
- Expansion limited by production/staffing capacity
- Vertical integration (Japanese carpenters, staff) provides control but constrains growth
- Debate: Cost-benefit of 100% authenticity in all markets

#### Staffing Sustainability
- Japanese staff essential to brand authenticity and operations
- Reliance on small pool of skilled Japanese workers
- Dependency on immigration/trade treaty arrangements

#### Bar Space Evolution
- Progressive increase in bar/lounge importance (18% to 33% of sales)
- Reflects learning about customer preferences

## Key Success Factors Summary

### Operational Efficiencies
1. Hibachi table model eliminating conventional kitchen
2. Limited menu reducing food waste and costs
3. Productive use of floor space (78% vs. typical 70%)
4. Japanese staff providing authentic atmosphere and showmanship

### Marketing Differentiation
1. Heavy advertising investment (8%-10% of sales)
2. Creative, unconventional marketing approach
3. Focus on visual/experiential product
4. Word-of-mouth emphasis (67% customer discovery)

### Customer Experience
1. Entertainment value (food preparation as show)
2. Atmosphere and authenticity
3. Service quality
4. High customer satisfaction (77.9% excellent on food; 71.3% excellent on service)

### Organizational Capability
1. Japanese paternalism combined with American entrepreneurialism
2. Low staff turnover and high commitment
3. Trained Japanese chefs as key competitive asset
4. Vertical integration of key operations (carpenters, staff sourcing)
